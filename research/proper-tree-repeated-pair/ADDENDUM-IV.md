# Independent repeated colors and separation only on actual edges

**Proposition.** Let T be a proper tree and let S be its nonempty weighted skeleton, with weights given by one plus the attached leaf count. Write W for the weight multiset and M for its maximum. Assume that the vertices whose weight occurs more than once form an independent set in S, and that the sum of weights at the ends of each actual skeleton edge is greater than M. Then the specialized first power-sum derivative Phi(T) determines T among proper trees. There is no restriction to one repeated pair and no separation condition on nonedges.

## Recovering triples from Phi

By [Awan, Proposition 2.1 and Lemma 2.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.13464v2#S2), use the forest formula and recover W and all colored edge counts e_R from Phi among proper trees, where R is an unordered two-weight multiset. Both hypotheses are checkable from these counts, so any competing proper tree with the same Phi also satisfies them. A proper-tree skeleton weight is at least 2. If the skeleton has fewer than three vertices, its weighted isomorphism type already follows from W and the edge counts; below assume k≥3.

For an available triple multiset C, write s=sum(C) and N_C for its connected induced triple count. If s−1≤M, no such triple is connected: an edge contributes at least M+1 and a third vertex at least 2. If C contains exactly two entries of repeated colors and one unique color, the unique vertex is necessarily the center of any connected triple. The count is the product of its two incident colored counts when the leaf colors differ, or the binomial coefficient choosing two from the incident count when they agree. Three repeated-color entries give zero.

For the remaining C with s−1>M, use the target monomial p_(s−1) multiplied by the small factors indexed by W minus C. Its factor count fixes two forest edges. The common sign in Awan's formula is (-1)^(sum(W)−k−1) because two selected edges contribute positive sign. Divide the target coefficient by this sign. If the two edges form one three-vertex component, marking it contributes (s−3)N_C, since the unmarked singleton multiset is exactly W minus C. If a singleton of weight b+1 was marked instead, the large component is unmarked; the target small factor b had to come from b+1. The triple multiset C' is obtained from C by replacing one b+1 by b. This contributes b times the multiplicity of b+1 in W minus C' times N_C'. Sum over distinct eligible b in W minus C for which C' exists. The sum of C' is s−1, so ascending induction applies; the previously covered cases can be evaluated directly. Here, a triple component has index >M even when marked, because it contains an actual edge and another vertex.

The remaining possibility is two disjoint edges. Their unmarked indices both exceed M, so one must be marked, and its index must fall to at most M. It therefore has endpoint multiset R of sum M+1, producing small index M. The other edge is unmarked and its weight sum must be s−1. Its endpoint multiset D is determined by balance: D=(C plus one M) minus R. Only include R if M appears among the target small factors, both endpoint multisets are defined, and R plus D is available in W. The weighted contribution is (M−1)e_R e_D. Indeed, R cannot contain M since weights are at least two. If R and D shared a weight, it would not be M and it would occur at least twice in C; those two occurrences would be repeated-color entries, contrary to the case under consideration. Thus R and D have disjoint color supports, so each pair of edges of those types is vertex-disjoint. This also rules out R=D. Sum over eligible marked types R; if an unordered forest can be marked in two ways, the forest formula counts the two markings separately. No extra factor counts placements of the small index M: it is a power-sum monomial, not an ordered list.

Subtract the disjoint-edge contribution and the previously known singleton-marked triple contributions, and divide by s−3>0. This recovers N_C. All selected target monomials avoid p1, so the specialization does not change these coefficient calculations.

## Reconstructing repeated vertices

Retain every unique-weight vertex and its edges to other unique-weight vertices, as read from colored edge counts. For each repeated weight a, make an auxiliary graph on the unique vertices in which a pair is adjacent if its two vertices have a common neighbor of weight a in S. For a nonadjacent pair in S this is decided by its connected triple count with color a; for an adjacent pair it is impossible by acyclicity. Each pair has at most one common neighbor in S, since two would make a four-cycle.

The neighborhood of every a-vertex of degree at least two is a clique in the auxiliary graph. Every clique of size at least two lies in such a neighborhood: fix an edge in the clique, which has a unique witness of weight a. For any third vertex in the clique, its two edges to that edge's ends must have the same witness as the fixed edge. Otherwise pair uniqueness forces all three witnesses to be distinct and produces a six-cycle. Consequently the maximal cliques of size at least two are precisely the neighborhoods of the a-vertices of degree at least two, each used once. Subtract their incidences at each unique vertex from the known incident edge count for a and add the indicated number of degree-one a-neighbors. There are no repeated-to-repeated edges by hypothesis, and no isolated skeleton vertices when k≥3. This recovers S up to weight-preserving isomorphism, then T by leaf expansion.

## Example and scope

The weights (8,10,11,9,12,9,16,2,16) at vertices 0 through 8 and edges 03,13,34,45,46,52,67,48 give a proper tree on 93 vertices and of diameter six. Both 9 and 16 have internal occurrences. The repeated-color vertices form an independent set, the minimum actual edge sum is 17, and the minimum available pair sum is 10, below M=16. The companion checker compares all 47 triple counts with the exact forest coefficient computation and reconstructs the repeated neighborhoods. The example checks are corroboration, not the proof.

Awan's Theorem 1 and [Zeng's Theorem 1.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18179) use the multiplicity-isolated condition, which this example violates. [Orellana and Tomba, Sections 4–5](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.18940v1#S4), and Awan's Section 3 treat uniform leaf extensions; uniform weights on a skeleton with an edge do not satisfy the independence hypothesis. The argument is a sufficient criterion among proper trees, does not claim unrestricted diameter-six reconstruction or the full conjecture, and makes no priority claim.
